Technologies

Under the crystal and inside the case hide treasures of ingenuity: to resist water, magnets and shocks, to draw energy from the sun, or to sync with atomic clocks.

Water resistance

A water-resistant watch is a tiny fortress. Every opening is sealed by a gasket, a soft rubber or silicone ring: under the crystal, under the case back and around the crown stem. A screw-down crown presses its gasket against the tube and closes the last door.

The deeper you go, the harder water pushes: at 10 metres down, the pressure is already twice that at the surface, and it rises by another bar every ten metres. Gaskets must hold, but also stay supple despite time, heat and salt.

The metres shown are a laboratory test pressure, not a depth to reach: movement in the water, a dive or a water jet create far higher pressures. And a watch is only water-resistant if its gaskets are in good condition.

The helium valve

Professional divers working very deep, on oil platforms for example, sometimes live for weeks in pressurised chambers where they breathe a mix containing helium. Helium atoms are so small that they eventually slip inside the watch, through the gaskets.

During decompression, the outside pressure drops faster than that of the helium trapped in the watch. With no way out, this gas could pop the crystal off. The helium valve, which appeared in the late 1960s, is a small vent that lets the gas escape, automatically or when unscrewed.

For recreational diving, it serves no purpose: it is professional equipment that has become a symbol of extreme dive watches.

Magnets and shocks

Anti-magnetism

Magnets are everywhere: speakers, phones, clasps on bags and cases. A magnetic field can make the coils of the hairspring stick together, and the watch suddenly gains several minutes a day.

To protect themselves, some watches enclose the movement in a soft-iron cage that deflects the magnetic field, like a Faraday cage. Others use silicon or special-alloy hairsprings that don’t react to magnets. The ISO 764 standard sets the minimum for a watch to call itself anti-magnetic.

Shock protection

The balance staff is thinner than a hair at its ends. A fall could break it. Since the 1930s, most watches mount the jewels of this staff in a small suspended system, held by a lyre-shaped spring: on impact, the jewel moves back, then returns exactly to its place.

Energy

Solar watches

Beneath a slightly translucent dial lies a photovoltaic cell. Light, from the sun or a lamp, passes through and produces a current that recharges a small accumulator. Once charged, a solar watch can run for months, sometimes longer, in total darkness, and there is never a battery to change.

There are also self-winding quartz watches, where a rotor like that of mechanical watches generates electricity, and hybrid movements where a spring drives the hands while a quartz regulates their speed.

Radio-controlled and GPS

A radio-controlled watch picks up a time signal every night, broadcast by large transmitters linked to atomic clocks, and resets itself. But these transmitters are in Europe, the United States, Japan and China: in Tahiti, no signal gets through.

The solution is GPS watches: GPS satellites carry atomic clocks themselves, and their signal covers the whole planet, Polynesia included. Your phone, for its part, sets itself through the network.

Atomic time

Since 1967, the second has no longer been defined by the Earth’s rotation, which is too irregular, but by the caesium-133 atom. One second is exactly 9,192,631,770 oscillations of the wave emitted by this atom as it changes energy state.

The most precise caesium atomic clocks would drift by less than one second in tens of millions of years. The new optical clocks, using strontium or ytterbium, do even better: less than one second over a span comparable to the age of the Universe. An average of hundreds of clocks around the world gives Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), the official time that everything else follows.

Because the Earth is slowing slightly, a “leap second” was added from time to time to stay in step with it. Scientists have decided to abandon them by 2035.

In the fenua

Salt and heat wear gaskets out faster than elsewhere. A few simple habits:

  • Have the water resistance checked regularly, especially before diving season, and have the gaskets replaced at each service.
  • Rinse the watch in fresh water after the sea, crown screwed down.
  • Don’t leave the watch in the sun on the dashboard or wear it in the sauna: heat hardens gaskets.
  • A solar watch loves our light, but not intense heat: better to recharge it by a window than in the full midday sun.
