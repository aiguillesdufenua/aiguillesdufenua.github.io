Calibres

The calibre is the heart of the watch. Behind the word hides a tiny city of wheels, springs and jewels, where every part has a precise role. Here is how it is organised, and how it turns a wound spring into a steady tick-tock.

What is a calibre?

The word “calibre” refers to a model of movement, with its shape, dimensions and layout of parts. It often carries a code name: NH35A, NH70A… Two very different watches can therefore share the same calibre.

Calibres are traditionally measured in lignes, an old unit worth about 2.26 mm: a 12-ligne calibre, like the NH35A, is just over 27 mm across. A house that designs and makes its own calibres speaks of an “in-house movement”; others use calibres made by specialists, like the ones I assemble.

The path of energy

In a mechanical calibre, energy always follows the same path, in five steps.

The crown or the rotor winds the spring enclosed in the barrel. As it unwinds, this spring turns a series of wheels, the gear train, one of which drives the hands. But left free, it would unwind in a few seconds: the escapement holds it back and only lets energy through in small pulses, to the rhythm of the balance and hairspring, which acts as a metronome.

The key parts

Here is a calibre seen from the back, without its rotor, followed by the role of each of its main parts.

  • The mainplatethe foundation

    The large base plate on which everything is built. It is drilled for the arbors and jewels, and supports the dial on the other side.

  • The bridgesthe framework

    Parts screwed onto the mainplate that hold the wheel arbors between them and the plate: the barrel bridge, the train bridge and the balance cock. These are the parts decorated with Côtes de Genève.

  • The barrelthe reservoir

    A small toothed drum that holds the mainspring, a coiled steel strip. On an automatic watch, a slipping bridle prevents overwinding. The ratchet wheel on top winds it, held by a click.

  • The gear trainthe transmission

    The series of wheels that transmits the energy: the centre wheel, which turns once an hour, the third wheel, the fourth wheel, then the escape wheel. Each wheel meshes with the pinion of the next and speeds up the motion.

  • The escapementthe gatekeeper

    The escape wheel and the pallet fork release the gear train tooth by tooth and give the balance a small push at each beat. This is what makes the tick-tock.

  • Balance and hairspringthe metronome

    A ring that swings on its staff, constantly pulled back by a very fine spring, the hairspring. Its steady frequency makes the watch accurate. A small regulator lengthens or shortens the hairspring to adjust the rate.

  • Jewels and shock protectionthe bearings

    Synthetic rubies act as bearings for the arbors to reduce friction. Those of the balance are spring-mounted to absorb shocks.

  • Winding and settingthe controls

    The winding stem carries a clutch wheel, guided by two levers, the setting lever and the yoke. With the crown pushed in, it winds the spring; pulled out, it turns the hands or changes the date.

  • The motion worksthe link to the hands

    On the dial side, the cannon pinion carries the minute hand; through the minute wheel, it drives the hour wheel twelve times more slowly, which carries the hour hand.

  • The oscillating weightautomatic winding

    The rotor turns with the movements of the wrist. A system of reversers, or of pawls as on NH calibres, turns its rotation in both directions into winding.

  • The date mechanismthe date

    A date disc runs around the movement, on the dial side. Once a day, around midnight, a finger pushes it one tooth, and a small spring, the jumper, holds it exactly in front of the window.

The lever escapement, in detail

Invented in the 18th century by Englishman Thomas Mudge, then perfected, the lever escapement is found today in almost every mechanical watch. Its little dance repeats hundreds of thousands of times a day.

  1. The balance swings. Its impulse pin enters the fork of the pallet fork and tips it over.

  2. As it tips, the pallet fork releases a tooth of the escape wheel, which moves forward one step, pushed by the spring.

  3. The tooth slides along the pallet stone and gives it a small push, which the fork passes on to the balance to keep it swinging.

  4. The other pallet stone stops the wheel at once. The balance swings back, and it all starts again the other way: tick… tock.

On an NH35A or NH70A, this happens 21,600 times an hour: six beats a second, hence the six small steps of the seconds hand each second.

In the fenua

A calibre’s oils age faster in heat and humidity. A regular service, roughly every four to six years, cleans the movement and renews its oils so it keeps ticking for a long time.
