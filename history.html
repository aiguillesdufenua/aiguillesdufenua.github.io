A history of time

From the sun’s shadows to smartwatches, five thousand years of ingenuity to measure what cannot be seen. Here are the great revolutions of watchmaking, and the workshops of genius that made them possible.

From 1500 BC

Reading time in the sky

The first clocks have no gears at all. In Egypt and Mesopotamia, people follow the shadow of a stick planted in the sun: the sundial. At night, or under cloudy skies, they use the clepsydra, a pierced vessel from which water drains at a steady rate.

Later, in Europe, the hourglass sets the rhythm of sailors’ watches and the length of sermons. Time is still measured with nature: sun, water, sand.

11th – 14th century

The first mechanical clocks

In 1088, in China, a huge astronomical tower driven by a waterwheel already cuts time into regular beats. In Europe, at the end of the 13th century, a decisive invention appears: the verge and foliot escapement.

A weight turns the wheels, and an oscillating bar, the foliot, releases them tooth by tooth. It is the original tick-tock. These clocks, set in cathedral towers and belfries, strike the hours for the whole town, but can drift by half an hour a day.

15th – 16th century

Time becomes portable

The invention of the mainspring changes everything: no more hanging weights, the energy now fits inside a box. In German cities, locksmith-clockmakers make the first portable clocks, then small watches worn as pendants.

They have a single hand, for the hours, and are more jewels than precise instruments. But for the first time, time travels with the person who wears it.

1656 – 1675

The precision revolution

Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens applies the pendulum to the clock, building on Galileo’s work. The error drops from about a quarter of an hour a day to a few seconds. The minute hand finally becomes useful.

Twenty years later, he invents the balance spring, a very fine spring that makes a watch’s balance wheel swing like a miniature pendulum. This balance and spring still beats today at the heart of every mechanical watch, including mine.

1714 – 1761

Finding the way at sea

At sea, knowing the exact time at the home port lets you calculate your longitude, and so your position. Without it, ships get lost and wreck. In 1714, the British Parliament promises a fortune to whoever solves the problem.

A self-taught carpenter, John Harrison, devotes his life to it. In 1761, his fourth timekeeper, the size of a large pocket watch, drifts by only a few seconds after weeks of sailing to Jamaica. The marine chronometer is born, and with it modern navigation.

1769 and 1773

Tahiti, a meeting point with time

Long before clocks, Polynesian navigators crossed the Pacific without instruments, reading the stars, the swell, the clouds and the flight of birds. A science of time and space passed down from generation to generation.

In 1769, European astronomers set up their clocks at Point Venus, in Matavai, to observe the transit of Venus across the Sun: the exact moments of contact had to be recorded to calculate the distance between the Earth and the Sun. And in 1773, during another expedition, a faithful copy of John Harrison’s timekeeper called at Tahiti aboard a ship that proved, around the world, that longitude could finally be calculated at sea.

18th – 19th century

The golden age of workshops

In the snowy valleys of the Jura, farming families become watchmakers during the long winters. In Geneva, Paris and London, masters invent most of what we still admire: around 1770, a watchmaker from Le Locle imagines the first watch that winds itself as its owner walks; in 1801, a genius from Neuchâtel working in Paris patents the tourbillon, a rotating cage that offsets the effects of gravity.

It is also the era when the great houses are born. The oldest manufacture still in uninterrupted operation is founded in Geneva in 1755. Others settle in the Vallée de Joux or on the shores of Lake Geneva and become legends. In the 19th century, American factories mass-produce watches with interchangeable parts, and the pocket watch becomes affordable for many.

1868 – 1931

The watch moves to the wrist

In 1868, a Geneva manufacture creates one of the first wristwatches for a Hungarian countess: at the time, it is a woman’s jewel. In 1904, a Parisian jeweller designs a watch to be worn on the wrist for a Brazilian aviator, who cannot let go of the controls to pull out his pocket watch.

World War I completes the shift: in the trenches, soldiers need to read the time at a glance. In 1926 the first truly waterproof case appears, and in 1931 the central rotor that turns a full 360°, the very one found in today’s automatic watches.

1953 – 1969

Ever further

Watches set off on adventures. In 1953 the first modern dive watches are born, with their rotating bezel and luminous indices. In 1960, a watch strapped to the outside of a bathyscaphe descends nearly 11,000 metres, to the bottom of the Mariana Trench, and comes back up intact.

And in July 1969, a hand-wound chronograph worn on the astronauts’ suits walks on the Moon. That same year, several houses unveil the first automatic chronographs.

1969 – 1983

The quartz revolution

On 25 December 1969, a Japanese house launches the first quartz wristwatch. It is a hundred times more accurate than a mechanical watch. Within a few years, prices collapse and digital displays take over wrists.

Swiss watchmaking then goes through the worst crisis in its history: tens of thousands of jobs disappear and many brands close. Salvation comes in 1983 from a colorful, inexpensive Swiss plastic watch made of only 51 parts, which brings a whole industry back to life.

Since the 1990s

Renaissance and new horizons

Against all odds, the mechanical watch is reborn. It is no longer needed to tell the time, so it becomes an object of passion: people love it for its living mechanism, its craftsmanship, its story. The great houses regain their prestige, and independent watchmakers reinvent the craft in small workshops.

Since 2015, smartwatches count our steps and heartbeats. And at the same time, reliable and affordable movements let enthusiasts all over the world assemble their own watches. That is exactly where my story begins.

And the rest of the story is written at the workbench.
