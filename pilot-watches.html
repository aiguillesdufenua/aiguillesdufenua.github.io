Pilot watches

In a cockpit, the time is not just information: it is a navigation instrument. Here is why pilot watches look the way they do, and how a slide rule found its way around a dial.

What a pilot watch is for

Before GPS, a pilot navigated by “dead reckoning”: knowing speed, heading and elapsed time, they calculated their position. A one-minute error could mean several kilometres. Every detail of a pilot watch answers a cockpit need.

  • Instant legibility

    Large numerals, broad hands, strong black-and-white contrast, luminous material: the time can be read at a glance, day or night, amid turbulence.

  • The triangle at twelve

    A triangle flanked by two dots marks the top of the dial, so you never misread it, even in a turn or in dim light.

  • A big crown

    Oversized, often onion-shaped, it can be operated with thick flying gloves.

  • Hacking seconds

    Before a mission, navigators synchronised their watches to the second, on a signal from the radio or the squadron leader.

  • Chronograph and flyback

    Timing a leg of the flight, a timed turn, fuel consumption; the flyback lets you chain measurements with a single push.

  • A second time zone

    Airline pilots follow universal time, “Zulu” time, as well as local time: hence the GMT hand.

A history at altitude

  1. 1904

    A watch for Santos-Dumont

    The Brazilian aviator cannot let go of the controls to pull out his pocket watch: his friend, jeweller Louis Cartier, designs him a watch to wear on the wrist.

  2. 1927

    Lindbergh crosses the Atlantic

    Thirty-three and a half hours of solo flight, navigating by dead reckoning: his feat shows how vital precise timekeeping is for navigating over the ocean.

  3. 1931

    The hour angle watch

    Designed with Charles Lindbergh, the Longines Hour Angle lets you calculate your longitude from the position of the stars, with a sextant and an almanac. Shortly before, US officer Philip Weems had devised a watch whose seconds dial could be turned to sync it with a radio signal.

  4. 1940

    Observation watches

    Luftwaffe navigators receive huge 55 mm watches, the “B-Uhren”, made to a common specification by five houses. Their type A, then type B dials still inspire a large share of pilot watches.

  5. 1948

    The Mark 11

    For Royal Air Force navigators, an accurate watch protected from magnets by a soft-iron cage.

  6. 1952

    The Navitimer

    A circular slide rule around a chronograph: the watch becomes a true flight computer. Its story is told in detail below.

  7. 1950s

    The Type 20 and the GMT

    The French air force orders flyback chronographs, the famous “Type 20”, from several houses including Breguet. In 1954, Rolex creates the GMT-Master for Pan Am crews, who cross time zone after time zone.

  8. 1962

    All the way to space

    A Navitimer with a 24-hour dial, the Cosmonaute, goes into orbit with Scott Carpenter.

Type A and type B

The two dials of 1940s observation watches have become classics.

Type A looks like a classic watch: large numerals from 1 to 11 and a triangle at twelve. Type B, which came next, reverses the priorities: the minutes, from 5 to 55, are shown large around the edge, and the hours on a small inner circle. For a navigator, the exact minute is what counts.

The Navitimer and its slide rule

Its name blends “navigation” and “timer”. Launched in 1952, it adds a circular slide rule to a chronograph, an idea the house had already tried in 1942. It was adopted by the US Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association, AOPA, and became pilots’ favourite tool before the era of electronic flight computers.

The principle is that of slide rules: two logarithmic scales, one on the rotating bezel, the other fixed around the dial. Turning the bezel amounts to multiplying and dividing. A marker, often red and placed at 60, serves as the index for time-related calculations.

What you can calculate with the bezel

  • Ground speed

    Distance flown on the outer scale opposite flight time on the inner scale: the speed is read opposite the 60 marker.

  • Flight time

    Set the speed opposite the 60 marker: opposite the remaining distance, read the time needed.

  • Fuel consumption

    Hourly consumption opposite the 60 marker: opposite the flight duration, read the fuel needed.

  • Conversions

    Special markers instantly convert statute miles, nautical miles and kilometres.

  • Rate of climb

    In feet per minute, to know how long it takes to reach an altitude.

  • Multiplying and dividing

    And any everyday calculation: it is a real slide rule on the wrist.

In the fenua

In a country made of over a hundred islands scattered across an area as large as Europe, the plane is a vital link. From the 1950s, seaplanes landed in the lagoons to connect the islands, long before Faa’a international airport opened in 1961. And long before them, Polynesian navigators were already crossing the Pacific by dead reckoning… without any watch.
