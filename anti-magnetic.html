Anti-magnetic watches

Power plants, laboratories, particle accelerators… and today phones and tablets: magnetism is the silent enemy of mechanical watches. Here is how watch engineers learned to tame it.

Why a magnet upsets a watch

The heart of a mechanical watch, the hairspring, is a steel spring thinner than a hair. In a magnetic field, it becomes magnetised: its coils attract each other and stick together. The hairspring effectively becomes shorter, the balance swings faster… and the watch starts gaining, sometimes several minutes a day.

Good news: it isn’t a breakdown. A watchmaker demagnetises a watch in seconds with a small device, and everything returns to normal.

Gauss, amperes per metre and teslas

The strength of a magnetic field is measured in gauss or in amperes per metre (A/m), about 80 A/m per gauss. For very strong fields, we use teslas: 1 tesla equals 10,000 gauss.

The ISO 764 standard requires a watch called “anti-magnetic” to withstand 4,800 A/m, about 60 gauss, without drifting by more than 30 seconds a day. Engineer’s watches go much further.

How to protect a watch

  • The soft-iron Faraday cage

    An inner soft-iron case surrounds the movement: it channels the magnetic field lines and diverts them from the hairspring. It is the historic solution, effective but adding weight and often ruling out a see-through case back.

  • Non-magnetic materials

    Since the 2000s, hairsprings made of silicon or special alloys, and parts in non-ferrous materials, no longer react to magnets. The watch no longer needs a cage, yet resists much stronger fields.

  • Demagnetisation

    If a watch is magnetised anyway, the watchmaker passes it for a few seconds through a demagnetiser, a device producing a decreasing alternating field. The hairspring is free again.

Engineer’s watches, from the laboratory to CERN

In the mid-20th century, electricity and physics spread through factories, power plants and laboratories. Engineers needed watches that could hold out.

  1. 1930

    The first anti-magnetic watches

    Tissot introduces one of the first mass-produced anti-magnetic wristwatches, long before the problem reached the general public.

  2. 1948

    The RAF navigators

    For the British air force’s navigators, sitting close to instruments and radars, IWC designs the Mark 11, whose movement is protected by a soft-iron cage.

  3. 1955

    The Ingenieur

    IWC applies the idea to an automatic watch for engineers and technicians: it withstands 80,000 A/m, about 1,000 gauss. In 1976, designer Gérald Genta gives it a new look, and a 1989 version reaches 500,000 A/m.

  4. 1956

    The Milgauss

    Its name says it all: a thousand gauss. Rolex intends it for scientists in power plants, laboratories and research centres. According to the brand, it was tested by engineers at CERN in Geneva.

  5. 1957

    The Railmaster

    Omega designs it for railway workers and technicians working near electrical installations. With the Seamaster 300 and the Speedmaster, it forms the brand’s professional “trilogy”.

  6. 2013

    The end of the cage

    Omega presents a watch resisting more than 15,000 gauss, or 1.5 tesla, with no cage at all, thanks to non-magnetic materials. That threshold later becomes one of the criteria of its “Master Chronometer” certification, checked by the Swiss metrology institute.

CERN, on the French-Swiss border near Geneva, houses the Large Hadron Collider, a ring 27 kilometres in circumference. Its superconducting magnets reach more than 8 teslas, over 80,000 gauss: an environment where an ordinary watch stands no chance.

Everyday life

You don’t need to work at CERN to magnetise your watch. Magnets hide everywhere around us.

  • Common sources

    Magnetic clasps on bags and phone or tablet cases, speakers, some laptops, induction hobs, magnetic bracelets and necklaces.

  • The compass test

    To find out if a watch is magnetised, bring it slowly near a compass: if the compass needle moves as it passes the watch, the watch is magnetised.

  • Good habits

    Don’t put your watch on a speaker or against a magnetic flap, and don’t store it in a magnetic case. If it suddenly gains time, think of magnetism first.
