The exterior

Watchmakers call the “habillage”, the dressing, everything around the movement: the case, the dial, the hands, the strap. It gives a watch its character, and it’s where most of my choices are made when I assemble a watch.

Case shapes

The shape of the case is the first thing you notice. Each one has its own history and temperament.

  • Round

    The most natural shape for a watch, since the hands turn in circles. Timeless, it goes with everything.

  • Cushion

    A square with very rounded corners and curved sides. Very fashionable from the 1910s to the 1930s, it often returns on dive watches.

  • Tonneau

    Sides curved like a wine barrel. An elegant, original shape that is hard to make, as the movement has to fit it.

  • Square

    Graphic and bold, the square shape appeals to Art Deco designers and aviation watches alike.

  • Rectangular

    Long and slim, often worn as a dress watch. It owes a lot to the 1910s and to the look of early tanks, which inspired its straight sides.

  • Octagonal

    Eight sides, often with a bezel whose screws remain visible. A powerful shape that became the emblem of luxury steel sports watches.

  • Oval

    Rarer, soft and precious, the oval is mostly found on ladies’ watches and jewellery pieces.

Materials

The case material changes the weight, the color, the feel, and how the watch ages.

  • 316L steel

    The benchmark stainless steel, used for the vast majority of watches. Strong and easy to polish.

    + tough, affordable, easy to repair− scratches over time

  • 904L steel

    A steel even richer in nickel, chromium and molybdenum, used by a few houses.

    + even better corrosion resistance, whiter sheen− harder to machine, more expensive

  • Titanium

    Almost half the weight of steel, it is popular for sports watches and sensitive skin.

    + very light, hypoallergenic, rustproof− greyer tone, marks easily

  • Bronze

    A copper and tin alloy that develops a patina over time: every watch becomes unique.

    + living patina, maritime charm− can turn green and stain the skin

  • Ceramic

    A technical ceramic fired at very high temperature, often black, used for bezels and cases.

    + practically scratch-proof, color never fades− can shatter under a hard impact

  • Forged carbon

    Carbon fibres compressed in resin, creating a marbled pattern different on every piece.

    + ultra-light, unique pattern− hard to repair

  • Yellow gold

    18-carat gold: 75% pure gold alloyed with silver and copper. The classic precious metal.

    + warm glow, rustproof, precious− heavy, soft, very expensive

  • Rose gold

    More copper in the alloy gives it this pinkish hue, very popular since the 2000s.

    + soft, warm tone− soft, very expensive

  • White gold

    Gold alloyed with white metals such as palladium, often coated with a thin layer of rhodium for shine.

    + discreet, looks like steel− rhodium plating can wear off

  • Platinum

    The noblest of watch metals, very dense and very rare. Houses sometimes mark it with a small, discreet detail.

    + the most prestigious, never tarnishes− very heavy, extremely expensive

Dials

The dial is the face of the watch. Its color and above all its texture change completely with the light.

  • Lacquered

    Several coats of lacquer applied and then polished, for a deep, mirror-like shine.

  • Sunburst

    Fine lines radiate from the centre: as you move your wrist, the light sweeps around the dial.

  • Guilloché

    A pattern engraved with a rose engine: tiny raised squares, waves, barleycorn… The noblest art of dial decoration.

  • Grained

    A finely grainy, matte surface, very soft to the eye, typical of vintage dials.

  • Grand feu enamel

    Glass powder melted in a kiln, layer after layer, at around 800 °C. A deep white that never ages, but with a risk of breakage at every firing.

  • Skeleton

    The dial disappears, or almost, revealing the wheels and bridges of the movement.

  • Sandwich

    Two stacked discs: the top one is cut out, the bottom one coated with luminous material. The numerals glow from below.

  • Mother-of-pearl

    A thin sliver of iridescent mother-of-pearl, unique on every watch. In the fenua, the black mother-of-pearl of pearl oysters gives it a very special flavour.

  • Aventurine

    A glass or stone sprinkled with tiny flecks that sparkle like a starry sky.

  • Meteorite

    A slice of iron meteorite, revealed with acid, showing interlaced crystal patterns formed in space.

A few processes

Electroplating
An electrolytic bath deposits a thin layer of metal or color on the dial: this is how many shades are obtained.
Pad printing
A silicone pad transfers the ink of numerals, logo and scales, with a precision of a few hundredths of a millimetre.
Applied indices
Metal indices and numerals, set one by one and fixed by tiny feet: they catch the light in relief.
Lume
Pigments that store light and release it in the dark, applied by hand or by machine on indices and hands.
Guilloché engraving
A rose engine, often a century old, guides a cutter along a template. Patterns are engraved line by line.
Sunburst and brushed finishes
Brushes or abrasive discs trace regular lines, radiating or parallel, that play with the light.

Hands

Hands must be legible at a glance while matching the style of the dial. Here are the most common shapes.

Baton
A simple bar, sober and modern.
Dauphine
An elongated diamond with a central ridge that plays with light; much loved on dress watches.
Leaf
Curved like a leaf, elegant and often vintage.
Sword
Shaped like a sword, wide and very legible, ideal for lume.
Alpha
Tapering from a wide base, between dauphine and sword.
Pomme
Slim, with a small ring near the tip; also called a Breguet hand, after the watchmaker who made it famous.
Cathedral
Pierced like a stained-glass window, typical of old military watches.
Mercedes
A three-pointed circle on the hour hand, to tell it apart in the dark.
Arrow
Ending in an arrowhead, often used for the GMT hand.
Syringe
A wide section extended by a thin tip, like a syringe; a vintage style.
Skeleton
Only the outline is drawn, letting the dial show through.

Hands are cut from thin metal sheets, then polished, gilded, painted or coated with lume. And sometimes, they go through fire.

Flame-blued hands

The deep blue of some hands doesn’t come from paint: it is the steel itself that changes color under heat. As it warms, an extremely thin oxide layer forms on its surface, and depending on its thickness, light reflects off it as pale straw, gold, brown, purple and then blue.

The watchmaker first polishes the hands to a mirror finish, since the slightest scratch would show under the blue. He lays them on a brass tray, often covered with filings to spread the heat, above a spirit lamp or a gentle torch. Then he watches, never taking his eyes off them: at around 290 °C, at the exact moment they turn midnight blue, he removes them and lets them cool. A few seconds too long, and the blue turns grey.

This natural blue has a depth and brilliance no paint can imitate, and its shade changes with the light. It is also fragile: a scratch reveals the steel again. Many hands today are blued by a chemical process or simply painted; flame bluing remains the mark of fine pieces. The blued screws in movements are treated the same way.

Straps and clasps

The strap completely changes the look of a watch. It’s also the easiest part to change yourself.

Oyster, three-row
Three rows of links, the two outer ones brushed and the central one wider: sturdy and sporty, the great classic of steel watches, introduced in the late 1940s.
Jubilee, five-row
Five rows, with small polished links in the centre: suppler, shinier, dressier. It was born in 1945 to celebrate an anniversary.
Milanese
A very fine woven mesh, supple like metal fabric; a retro, elegant style.
Rubber
Supple, waterproof and comfortable; the best are fluoroelastomer (FKM), which resists seawater, UV and heat.
Leather
Warm and elegant, more beautiful over time… as long as it stays dry.
NATO
A nylon strap that passes under the watch, of military origin: strong, colorful, easy to change and quick to dry.

The names Oyster and Jubilee come from a great Geneva house; by extension, they have become the names of these bracelet styles, now found at many brands.

Pin buckle
Like a belt: simple and universal.
Deployant clasp
Folding blades: the strap doesn’t wear out and the watch can’t fall when opened.
Micro-adjust
A system that lengthens the bracelet by a few millimetres without tools, very handy when the wrist swells in the heat.

In the fenua

Heat, humidity, salt and sweat: our climate is tough on watches, and especially on straps.

  • Leather is the least suitable: it soaks up sweat and water, loses its shape, ends up smelling and can grow mould. Best kept for special occasions.
  • Steel, titanium and FKM rubber are the best suited: they don’t mind water or salt.
  • NATO straps dry quickly and are easy to wash.

After a swim in the sea, a simple rinse in fresh water, with the crown closed, stops salt from building up in the links and gaskets. As for cases, titanium and steel age very well in the tropics; bronze develops its patina faster in salty air.
