The watchmaker’s equipment

Beyond screwdrivers and tweezers, a watchmaker’s workshop has a few devices that diagnose a watch without even opening it: listening to it tick, demagnetising it, checking that it is water-resistant. Here is how they work, and how to read what they say.

The demagnetiser

It is often the first device to come out when a watch suddenly gains several minutes a day. A small box produces an alternating magnetic field: you place the watch on it, press the button, then slowly move it away, or release the button, depending on the model.

The field reverses dozens of times a second while weakening: it “shakes” the hairspring’s magnetic domains until they are left disordered, that is, unmagnetised. It takes a few seconds. You then check with a compass, or directly on the timegrapher: the rate should return to normal.

The timegrapher

The timegrapher, also called a timing machine, is a very sensitive microphone connected to a small computer. You clamp the watch in its holder: it listens to the tick-tock, compares it to a very precise reference quartz, and shows the movement’s health in a few seconds.

Each tick is actually a series of three small sounds made by the escapement. By measuring the precise moment of each, the device works out everything else.

  • 1. Unlocking

    The balance’s impulse pin enters the fork and unlocks the pallet fork: the escape wheel is released.

  • 2. Impulse

    A tooth of the wheel slides along the pallet stone, and the fork pushes the balance: this is the impulse.

  • 3. Drop

    The escape wheel stops sharply against the other pallet stone: the loudest sound.

  • Rate (s/d)

    How fast or slow the watch runs, in seconds per day, extrapolated from a few seconds of listening. It is the measure of accuracy.

  • Amplitude (°)

    The angle the balance travels from its rest position before turning back. It reflects the movement’s “vigour”: low amplitude points to a poorly wound spring, aged oils or wear.

  • Beat error (ms)

    The time difference, in milliseconds, between “tick” and “tock”. If the hairspring’s rest position isn’t perfectly aligned with the line of centres, the balance takes slightly longer one way than the other.

  • Lift angle (°)

    Not a measurement but a setting: the angle during which the impulse pin stays in contact with the fork, specific to each calibre. The device uses it to calculate amplitude from the impulse duration: 53° for an NH35.

  • Frequency (BPH)

    The number of beats per hour, detected automatically: 21,600 for my calibres, six beats a second.

Reading the results

Here are general guidelines for a calibre like the NH35, measured fully wound. Each calibre has its own tolerances.

ReadingVery goodFineTo watch
Ratewithin ±5 s/dwithin manufacturer tolerance (−20 to +40 s/d)beyond
Amplitude (dial up)270 to 310°250 to 270°below 220°
Beat errorbelow 0.5 ms0.5 to 1 msabove 1.5 ms
Positional variationbelow 10 s/d10 to 20 s/dabove 30 s/d

The watch is measured in several positions: dial up, dial down, then crown up, down, left and right. Amplitude is slightly lower in vertical positions, and the rate varies from one position to another: the positional variation. Certified chronometers are tested in five positions and at three temperatures.

On an NH35, the rate is adjusted with the regulator, a small lever that lengthens or shortens the active part of the hairspring, and beat error with the movable stud carrier, which shifts the hairspring’s attachment point. Delicate work, done with a very fine screwdriver and great care.

Water resistance

A water-resistant watch only stays that way if its gaskets are in good condition. Before a watch goes back in the water, the watchmaker checks its water resistance with devices that, for the most part, don’t even expose the watch to water.

  • The dry air tester

    The watch is placed under a bell where pressure, then vacuum, is applied. A sensor measures the tiny deformation of the crystal and case: if it doesn’t stabilise, air is getting in or out, and the watch leaks. No risk to the movement.

  • The underwater test

    Dive watches are immersed in a pressurised tank, to their rated depth and beyond. They can also be submerged under vacuum: a stream of bubbles betrays the leak.

  • The condensation test

    The watch is gently heated to about 40–45 °C, then a drop of cold water is placed on the crystal. If fog appears inside, moisture has got in.

  • Gaskets and grease

    Replacing the gaskets, lightly greased with silicone, is part of every service. A hardened or crushed gasket is the most common cause of leaks.

Other workshop equipment

  • The cleaning machine

    Movement parts go through successive cleaning and rinsing baths, sometimes agitated by ultrasound, then are dried with warm air.

  • Oilers and oils

    Tiny needles place a drop of oil or grease, different for each friction point.

  • The staking set

    A small frame with stakes and punches, to push out or refit press-fitted parts: jewels, arbors, hands.

  • The crystal press

    To fit or remove a crystal set in the bezel without breaking it.

  • The watchmaker’s lathe

    A miniature lathe to make or touch up arbors and parts to a hundredth of a millimetre.

  • The winding test bench

    A winder that turns automatic watches for days, to check their rate over time.

In the fenua

With heat, salt and dives in the lagoon, a water-resistance test is a yearly habit here, and before every diving season. Papeete’s watchmakers do it in a few minutes.
