Part one

In the manufacture

A mechanical watch often has more than a hundred parts, some of them thinner than a hair. Here is the path they travel before ticking on a wrist.

  1. Design

    It all starts with drawing: the style of the watch, then the plans of the movement, designed part by part on a computer. Every wheel and every spring is calculated to a hundredth of a millimetre.

  2. Components

    Plates and bridges are machined from brass, arbors and pinions turned on bar-turning machines, wheels cut one by one. The hairspring, a special alloy wire thinner than a hair, is a craft of its own; some are now made of silicon. The jewels are synthetic rubies.

  3. Finishing

    Parts are decorated: Côtes de Genève, perlage, polished bevels, screws blued by heating them to around 290 °C. In industrial production, it is all done by machine; in haute horlogerie, by hand, even on parts no one will ever see.

  4. Movement assembly

    Watchmakers, or robots for large series, assemble the barrel, the gear train, the escapement and the balance. Each friction point gets a tiny drop of oil, in exactly the right place.

  5. Regulation

    The movement is placed on a device that listens to its ticking and measures how fast or slow it runs, in several positions. The watchmaker adjusts the balance until the rate is steady.

  6. Dial and hands

    The dial is a thin metal plate, lacquered, painted or textured, onto which the indices are printed or applied, then the luminous material. The hands are cut, polished, sometimes blued or painted.

  7. Case and crystal

    The case is forged and then machined from stainless steel, and its surfaces are polished or brushed. Sapphire crystal comes from a synthetic crystal, cut and polished with diamond tools, then often given an anti-reflective coating.

  8. Casing and testing

    The movement, dressed with its dial and hands, is placed in the case. Then come the tests: water resistance, rate over several days. Some watches are certified as chronometers after fifteen days of testing, in five positions and three temperatures.

Part two

At my workbench

I don’t make the parts myself: I choose existing components and assemble them. It’s the best school for understanding a watch, and every assembly teaches me a new skill.

First of all: compatible parts

The movement
Everything starts with it. I use very common Japanese automatic movements, such as the Seiko NH35A, for which many matching parts exist.
The case
It must be designed for that exact movement, with the right opening for the stem and, if needed, a movement spacer ring to hold it in place.
The dial
Its diameter, its feet and the position of the date window must match the movement and the case.
The hands
Their holes must have the right size: on an NH35, 1.50 mm for the hours, 0.90 mm for the minutes and 0.20 mm for the seconds. Their length must suit the dial.

My tools

A few tools are enough to start, as long as they are of good quality and you work somewhere clean and well lit.

  • A watchmaker’s loupe: held against the eye and magnifying three to five times, to check hand alignment and hunt down the tiniest speck of dust.
  • Watchmaker’s screwdrivers: fine and of good quality, so as not to damage screw heads.
  • Tweezers: very fine, ideally plastic or brass so they don’t scratch.
  • A hand setter: to press the hands on perfectly straight without marking the dial.
  • A case back wrench and a press: to open and close screwed or snap-on case backs.
  • Precision flush cutters: to cut the winding stem to the right length, cleanly and without crushing it.
  • An air blower: to chase away the tiniest speck of dust before closing.
  • Rodico putty: a soft putty that picks up dust and removes fingerprints without leaving residue; it also helps pick up a tiny part.
  • An anti-static watchmaker’s mat: which stops parts from rolling or bouncing, protects what lies on it and limits the static electricity that attracts dust.
  • Finger cots or gloves: to never leave fingerprints on the dial.
  • A movement holder and a dial protector: to keep the movement flat and protect the dial while fitting the hands.

My assembly steps

  1. Prepare the workbench

    The anti-static mat rolled out on a clear surface, a good lamp, the loupe and tools within reach. I also check that the movement works before starting: a few turns of the crown, and the balance should start beating.

  2. Fit the dial

    The movement sits in its holder. The dial is fixed by its feet or with small adhesive dots, making sure the date window lines up exactly with the date.

  3. Fit the hands

    This is the trickiest step. I move the time forward until the date has just changed: it is then exactly midnight. I press the hour hand right on 12, then the minute hand, then the seconds hand, checking that they don’t touch and turn freely.

  1. Case the movement

    The movement, with its spacer ring if the case needs one, is placed in the case, dial facing the crystal. One last check with the loupe and a puff of the air blower before going further.

  2. Adjust the stem and crown

    The original stem is almost always too long. I push it into the cased movement, then hold the crown beside it, in its final place against the tube: the bottom of its threaded hole tells me where to make a mark. I pull the stem out by pressing the movement’s small release lever, and cut it with precision flush cutters, a little longer than the mark, holding the end between my fingers so it doesn’t fly off. I then deburr the end with a fine file, otherwise the crown screws on badly and its thread gets damaged. I put it all back and test: the crown must sit against the case and the clicks must stay crisp. Too long, I trim a little more; too short, I need a new stem. Hence the rule: cut little, test, repeat.

  1. Close the case back

    The gasket goes in, lightly greased, then the case back is screwed on with the wrench or pressed on with the press, depending on the model.

  2. Test

    I let the watch run for at least a day to check that it keeps time, that the date changes around midnight and that the hands don’t rub.

  3. Fit the strap

    The final touch: the strap, held between the lugs by its spring bars. The watch is ready.

My beginner’s tips

  • Take your time. Rushing is the number one cause of scratched dials and bent hands.
  • Chase the dust. A speck of dust under the crystal shows every day: always check with the loupe, puff some air and, if needed, dab with Rodico before closing.
  • Never touch the dial. Finger cots, a dial protector, and hands handled by their tube, never by their tip.
  • Cut the stem little by little. You can always cut more, never add length back.
  • Don’t promise water resistance. Without a pressure test, a home-assembled watch shouldn’t go underwater.

Every one of my watches has gone through these steps.
