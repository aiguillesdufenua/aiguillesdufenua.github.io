Legendary watches

Some watches are far more than mechanisms: they carry a story, a feat, a bold idea or a love. Here are some of the finest, followed by a look at the great Swiss houses and Japanese watchmaking.

The great stories

Watches that marked their era, told in chronological order.

  • 1770 – 1931

    The first automatic watches

    In the 1770s, Swiss watchmaker Abraham-Louis Perrelet, from Le Locle, developed one of the first pocket watches wound by the motion of walking; Breguet later perfected the idea with his “perpétuelle” watches. On the wrist, Englishman John Harwood patented an automatic wristwatch in 1923, sold from 1926 with Fortis: its weight only swings back and forth between two stops. In 1931, Rolex introduced a rotor turning freely through 360°, the principle of almost every automatic calibre today, including the NH35 in my watches.

  • 1783 – 1827

    Breguet’s Marie-Antoinette

    In 1783, an admirer of the queen commissions from Abraham-Louis Breguet a watch with every known complication, with no limit of time or cost. The queen dies on the scaffold in 1793, Breguet in 1823: his son completes it in 1827, forty-four years after the order. Stolen in 1983 from a museum in Jerusalem, it was only recovered in 2007.

  • 1810 – 1868

    The first wristwatches

    In 1810, Caroline Murat, Queen of Naples and Napoleon’s sister, ordered from Breguet a watch to wear on the wrist, delivered in 1812: it is the oldest known wristwatch. In 1868, Patek Philippe made one for a Hungarian countess. For decades, the wristwatch would remain a woman’s jewel, before aviators and soldiers adopted it.

  • 1904

    The Cartier Santos

    Brazilian aviator Alberto Santos-Dumont tells his friend Louis Cartier he cannot let go of his airship’s controls to pull out his pocket watch. Cartier designs him a square watch to wear on the wrist, with visible screws. It isn’t the very first wristwatch, but it is one of the first men’s watches designed for the wrist, and it paved the way for pilot watches.

  • 1912

    The Titanic watches

    Pocket watches of the time weren’t water-resistant: the icy water stopped those of the victims, and several were found stopped around the time of the sinking. The most famous belonged to Isidor Straus, co-owner of Macy’s department stores, who refused to board a lifeboat before the women and children; his wife Ida refused to leave him. His gold watch, a gift from Ida for his 43rd birthday, stopped at 2:20, the moment the liner disappeared. It sold in 2025 for 1.78 million pounds, a record for Titanic memorabilia.

  • 1917 – 1928

    The “Radium Girls”

    During the First World War, demand for luminous dials soared. In the United States, young women painted indices and hands with radium paint; they were taught to point their brushes between their lips, “lip, dip, paint”. The radium settled in their bones: many suffered terrible jaw necrosis and cancers. Their legal battle in the late 1920s advanced the recognition of occupational diseases. Radium was later replaced by tritium, then Super-LumiNova, as explained on the Technologies page.

  • 1917

    The Tank

    Louis Cartier draws on the outline of First World War tanks, seen from above, to design a rectangular watch with parallel side bars. It becomes one of the most famous and most imitated dress watches.

  • 1926

    The Rolex Oyster and the Channel swim

    Hans Wilsdorf patents a water-resistant case, with screwed crown and back, sealed like an oyster. To prove it, he gives an Oyster to swimmer Mercedes Gleitze, who attempts another Channel crossing in October 1927: she has to give up after more than ten hours in icy water, but the watch works perfectly. Wilsdorf turns it into a famous advertisement.

  • 1931

    The Reverso

    In British India, officers playing polo keep breaking the crystal of their watches. Jaeger-LeCoultre devises a case that swivels and flips over to show its metal back during the match.

  • 1933

    The Henry Graves Supercomplication

    Two American collectors, banker Henry Graves Jr. and industrialist James Ward Packard, compete for the world’s most complicated watch. Graves wins with this 24-complication Patek Philippe pocket watch, which remained the world’s most complicated until 1989; it is still the most complicated ever designed without the help of computers. It sold for over 23 million Swiss francs in 2014.

  • 1943 – 1944

    The prisoner-of-war Rolexes

    Captured British officers often had their watches confiscated. Rolex founder Hans Wilsdorf offered to let them order one from their camp and pay only after the war: about 3,000 watches were sent. In 1943, at Stalag Luft III, Corporal Clive Nutting ordered an Oyster chronograph; according to an often-repeated account, it was used to time the guards’ rounds while preparing the “Great Escape” of March 1944, when 76 prisoners fled through a tunnel. Nutting did pay for his watch after the war, and his correspondence with Wilsdorf survives.

  • 1952

    The Breitling Navitimer

    Its name blends “navigation” and “timer”. Around a chronograph, a circular slide rule lets the pilot calculate speed, flight time or fuel consumption in the air. Adopted by the US Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association, AOPA, it became the cockpit instrument before the era of electronic flight computers; in 1962, its 24-hour version, the Cosmonaute, went into orbit with Scott Carpenter. How its slide rule works is explained in detail on the Pilot watches page.

  • 1953

    The Rolex Submariner

    Developed with the help of diver René-Paul Jeanneret, it set the codes of the dive watch for decades: a graduated rotating bezel to time immersion, large luminous indices, 100 metres of water resistance at the time. Its story and its rivals’ are told on the Dive watches page.

  • 1957

    Elvis Presley’s Hamilton Ventura

    The world’s first electric watch, powered by a battery, in a futuristic asymmetric triangular case. In 1961, Elvis Presley wears it on screen in Blue Hawaii, filmed in Hawaii, our Pacific neighbour.

  • 1959

    The Seiko Alpinist

    Designed for Japanese mountaineers, the “yama-otoko”, it was meant to be sturdy and legible. It is often described as Seiko’s first sports watch, the ancestor of a long line of adventure watches.

  • 1963

    The Sea-Gull 1963

    In 1961, the Chinese government gives the Tianjin watch factory a secret mission, “Project 304”: to create China’s first chronograph for air force pilots. The factory buys the tooling of a Swiss movement, the Venus 175; the prototypes are ready in 1963, hence the name given by collectors. Reissued today, it has become a cult watch for its gentle price and column-wheel movement.

  • 1969

    The race for the automatic chronograph

    Until 1969, every chronograph was hand-wound. That year, three teams competed to make the first automatic chronograph: Zenith unveiled the El Primero in January; a consortium of Heuer, Breitling, Hamilton-Buren and Dubois-Dépraz revealed its Calibre 11, the one in the Monaco; and Seiko began selling its 6139 calibre in Japan in May, combining a column wheel and a vertical clutch. Who was truly first? Enthusiasts still debate it.

  • 1969 – 1984

    The Zenith El Primero, saved by an attic

    The El Primero beats at 36,000 vibrations per hour, a very high frequency that measures tenths of a second. In 1975, at the height of the quartz crisis, Zenith’s management decided to stop mechanical movements and get rid of the tooling. An employee, Charles Vermot, secretly hid the plans, tools and cams in an attic of the manufacture. In 1984, when mechanics came back into favour, this treasure allowed production to restart at once. From 1988 to 2000, a modified El Primero even powered the Rolex Daytona. It still beats today.

  • 1969

    Steve McQueen’s Heuer Monaco

    A square case, a first for a water-resistant chronograph, and one of the very first automatic chronographs. In 1971, Steve McQueen wears it in the film Le Mans, in a racing suit modelled on that of Jo Siffert, a Heuer-sponsored driver: the watch becomes a motorsport icon.

  • 1969

    The Moonwatch

    The hand-wound chronograph qualified by NASA in 1965 after extreme trials, a Speedmaster 105.012 on Buzz Aldrin’s wrist on the Moon, then the Apollo 13 crew’s ally. Its whole story is told on the Watches in space page.

  • 1969

    The Seiko Quartz Astron

    On 25 December 1969, Seiko began selling the first quartz wristwatch in Tokyo: a tuning-fork crystal and a stepper motor gave it an accuracy of about five seconds a month, far beyond the best mechanics. Seiko made its patents available to other makers: the quartz watch spread across the world, and the “quartz crisis” shook Swiss watchmaking for over a decade.

  • Late 1960s – 2017

    Paul Newman’s Daytona

    The actor and racing driver receives from his wife, Joanne Woodward, a Rolex Daytona chronograph engraved “Drive carefully me”. He wears it for years. Sold at auction in 2017 for 17.8 million dollars, it then becomes the most expensive wristwatch ever sold.

  • 1971

    David Scott’s Bulova

    During the Apollo 15 mission, the crystal of commander David Scott’s Speedmaster popped off during his second moonwalk. For the third, he strapped on a Bulova chronograph prototype he had brought along, a watch “not authorised” by NASA. Still his property, covered in lunar dust, it sold for 1.625 million dollars in 2015. The full story is on the Watches in space page.

  • 1972

    The Royal Oak, drawn in one night

    In the early 1970s, on the eve of a Basel fair, Audemars Piguet asks designer Gérald Genta for a new kind of steel sports watch. Legend has it he draws it in a single night, inspired by the porthole of a diving helmet: an octagonal bezel with eight screws, a “tapisserie” dial, an integrated bracelet. Unveiled in 1972 and priced above many gold watches, it is called mad; it invents the luxury sports watch. Genta did it again in 1976 with Patek Philippe’s Nautilus.

  • 1983

    The Casio G-Shock

    Engineer Kikuo Ibe set himself the “triple 10” goal: survive a 10-metre fall, 10 bar of pressure, and last 10 years on one battery. He threw more than 200 prototypes out of the office restroom window, until a child playing with a ball in a park gave him the idea: suspend the module inside a shock-absorbing shell. The G-Shock became the watch of soldiers, divers… and astronauts.

The Holy Trinity of Swiss watchmaking

This is the name given to the three most prestigious Swiss houses, all independent and founded in the 18th or 19th century. Their pieces are the benchmark of haute horlogerie.

  • Vacheron Constantin

    Geneva, 1755

    The elder: founded by watchmaker Jean-Marc Vacheron, it is the oldest manufacture in uninterrupted operation. It is famed for extreme complications and supremely refined finishing.

  • Patek Philippe

    Geneva, 1839

    Founded by Polish émigré Antoni Patek, joined by French watchmaker Adrien Philippe. Its grand complications and auction records make it, for many, the most prestigious house of all.

  • Audemars Piguet

    Le Brassus, 1875

    Founded in the Vallée de Joux by Jules Louis Audemars and Edward Auguste Piguet, it still belongs to the founding families. A master of complications, it also invented the luxury sports watch with the Royal Oak.

Two other exceptional houses are often added: A. Lange & Söhne, in Saxony, and Breguet.

Grand Seiko, the Japanese challenge

In 1960, Seiko launched Grand Seiko with a clear ambition: to make the best possible watch, able to rival the best of Switzerland. In 1967, designer Taro Tanaka set out its style: flat surfaces and sharp edges, mirror-polished so that light plays on every facet.

This “zaratsu” polish, done by hand on a spinning disc, leaves surfaces without the slightest distortion. Dials draw on Japanese nature, like the white birch forest near the Shizukuishi studio, at the foot of Mount Iwate. Its mechanical movements are regulated to an in-house standard stricter than that of Swiss chronometers, between −3 and +5 seconds a day.

In 1999, Seiko invented Spring Drive: a spring drives the hands, but a quartz regulates their speed, giving a seconds hand that glides without the slightest jerk. Grand Seiko became an independent brand in 2017.

Swiss and Japanese watchmaking

Two great traditions, two philosophies. These differences are general trends: there are remarkable exceptions on both sides.

In SwitzerlandIn Japan
OrganisationA network of houses, movement makers and specialist suppliers, often within large groupsLarge, highly integrated houses that make almost everything themselves, from hairspring to crystal and lume
ImageHeritage, prestige, haute horlogerie and its complicationsReliability, precision and excellent value
InnovationGrand complications, the tourbillon, materials such as siliconQuartz in 1969, solar watches, Spring Drive
FinishingCôtes de Genève, perlage, hand bevelling, the Geneva Seal“Zaratsu” mirror polish, dials inspired by nature and the seasons
Labels“Swiss Made”, requiring at least 60% of the value to be Swiss, and COSC chronometer certificationBrand-specific accuracy standards, such as Grand Seiko’s

My own watches run on Japanese calibres: tough, reliable and affordable, they are the ideal school for learning.
