Complications

In watchmaking, a “complication” is any function that goes beyond hours, minutes and seconds. From the simple date to the minute repeater, here is a full inventory of these mechanisms, from the most common to the most extraordinary.

  • Astronomy
  • Timing
  • Chiming
  • Time zones
  • Displays
  • Precision

Each complication carries a complexity rating, from 1 dot (common) to 5 dots (reserved for the greatest masters).

Calendar complications

They show the date, and sometimes much more: the day, the month, the year… even leap years.

  • Date

    The most common complication: a disc numbered 1 to 31 appears in a window. It always counts to 31 and must be corrected by hand five times a year, after short months.

  • Big date

    Two separate discs, one for tens and one for units, show the date in large, much more legible numerals.

  • Day-date

    A second disc shows the day of the week, often spelled out, next to the date.

  • Complete calendar

    Date, day and month together on the dial, often with a moon phase. Like the simple date, it doesn’t know the length of the months.

  • Annual calendar

    Introduced in the 1990s, it can tell 30-day months from 31-day ones. It needs only one correction a year, at the end of February.

  • Perpetual calendar

    Its French names, “quantième perpétuel” and “calendrier perpétuel”, mean the same thing. A cam turning once every four years lets it know the length of every month, leap years included. Properly set, it shows the right date without any intervention until 2100.

  • Secular calendar

    The ultimate refinement: it also knows that century years, such as 2100, are only leap years if divisible by 400. Only a handful of watches can do it.

Astronomical complications

They link the watch to the sky: the Moon, the Sun, the tides.

  • Moon phase

    A disc painted with two moons moves a little every day behind a hill-shaped opening. The lunar cycle lasts 29 days, 12 hours and 44 minutes: a simple mechanism falls a day behind roughly every two and a half years, the most precise only every 122 years, or even far longer.

  • Equation of time

    It shows the gap between watch time and solar time, which varies over the year from about −14 to +16 minutes. A bean-shaped cam, turning once a year, calculates it.

  • Tide indicator

    Guided by the course of the Moon, it shows high and low tide. A complication for sailors and fishermen.

  • Day-night indicator

    A small disc with a sun and a moon shows whether it is day or night, very handy to know if a time in another zone is morning or evening.

Timing

Chronograph complications, to time a race, a recipe or a dive.

  • Chronograph

    Two pushers start, stop and reset a central seconds hand, while sub-dials count the elapsed minutes and hours.

  • Flyback

    A single push resets the chronograph and restarts it at once, without stopping it first. A function born for pilots timing one leg after another.

  • Split-seconds (rattrapante)

    Two stacked seconds hands start together; one can be stopped to read an intermediate time, then made to “catch up” with the other. One of the most delicate complications.

  • Tachymeter, telemeter, pulsometer

    Scales around the dial turn the chronograph into an instrument: speed over a kilometre, the distance of a storm using the speed of sound, or heartbeats.

  • Deadbeat seconds

    On a mechanical watch, the seconds hand usually sweeps in small steps. Deadbeat seconds make it jump once a second, like a quartz… but by mechanics alone.

Chiming complications

The complications that make the watch sing: the most poetic, and among the most difficult.

  • Alarm

    An extra hand sets the alarm time; a small hammer then strikes a membrane or a gong. The first mechanical alarm wristwatches appeared in the 1940s.

  • Minute repeater

    A slide on the side of the case winds a spring; the watch then chimes the exact time: low notes for the hours, “ding-dongs” for the quarters, high notes for the minutes. Born to tell the time in the dark, before electricity.

  • Grande and petite sonnerie

    Like a church clock on the wrist: the watch chimes the hours and quarters by itself as they pass. The grande sonnerie repeats the hour at every quarter, the petite only on the hour. Some even play a carillon on four gongs.

Time zones

For travellers, or to keep an eye on the time of loved ones on the other side of the world.

  • GMT or dual time

    An extra hand goes around the dial in 24 hours and shows a second time zone, often read on a bezel marked 1 to 24.

  • World time

    A ring of 24 cities and a 24-hour disc turn together: at a glance, you can read the time in every zone of the world.

Original displays

Or how to read the time other than with two hands.

  • Power reserve indicator

    A small hand shows the energy left in the spring, like a fuel gauge. You know when to wind your watch.

  • Jumping hours

    The hours appear as numerals in a window and change in one jump, right on the hour, while a hand or disc shows the minutes.

  • Retrograde display

    A hand travels along an arc, then springs back to its starting point. Used for the date, the minutes or the days, it makes a striking show.

  • Regulator

    Inspired by observatory clocks: the large central hand shows the minutes, while hours and seconds each have their own small dial, for unambiguous reading.

  • Wandering hours

    An hour numeral travels along an arc marked in minutes, then gives way to the next. A 17th-century invention for night clocks.

Precision complications

They add no display: they improve the regularity of the rate, and are often shown off as a spectacle.

  • Tourbillon

    The whole escapement and balance turn in a cage, usually once a minute, to offset the effects of gravity depending on the watch’s position. Invented in 1801, it has become the symbol of haute horlogerie.

  • Constant force

    A fully wound spring pushes hard, an almost unwound one pushes weakly, which harms precision. A fusee and chain, or a small intermediate spring, deliver an always-equal force to the balance.

The “grand complication”

A watch combining at least a perpetual calendar, a minute repeater and a chronograph, often a split-seconds one, earns the title of grand complication. It can contain more than a thousand parts and take a single watchmaker several years.

The most complicated watch ever made now combines more than sixty complications. The most beautiful are not necessarily the most complicated: a well-drawn moon phase has its own poetry too.

In the fenua

A tide indicator would be of little use in Tahiti! Our tides are very small, barely a few tens of centimetres, and they follow the Sun rather than the Moon: the sea is usually high around noon and midnight. Here, any watch… already tells the tides.
