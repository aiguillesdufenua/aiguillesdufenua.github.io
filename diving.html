Dive watches

From the waters of our lagoons to the great depths, humans learned to breathe underwater, and had to measure their time as carefully as their air. Here are the pioneers, the watches that became legends, and the tools that took over: tables, then computers.

The pioneers of the silent world

  1. 1926

    Commander Le Prieur’s diving apparatus

    French naval officer Yves Le Prieur develops, with Maurice Fernez, a self-contained compressed-air apparatus: for the first time, a diver is freed from the hose linking him to the surface. But the air flows continuously, and the cylinder empties fast.

  2. 1943

    Cousteau, Gagnan and the regulator

    Naval officer Jacques-Yves Cousteau and engineer Émile Gagnan invent the demand regulator, which only delivers air on inhalation, at the surrounding water pressure. Modern scuba is born. With Philippe Tailliez and Frédéric Dumas, the “mousquemers”, Cousteau makes dive after dive in the Mediterranean.

  3. 1950

    The Calypso

    Cousteau turns a former minesweeper into an exploration ship, the Calypso, which will sail the oceans for decades.

  4. 1956

    The Silent World

    Co-directed with Louis Malle, the film reveals the seabed to the general public. It wins the Palme d’Or at Cannes, then the Oscar for best documentary. Elsewhere, Austrian Hans Hass also popularises diving through his films.

  5. 1962

    Living under the sea

    With the Précontinent underwater habitats, the Cousteau team has “oceanauts” live for days, then weeks, on the seabed.

Legendary dive watches

As divers went deeper, they needed a reliable instrument to keep track of their time underwater. Here are the models that made history.

  • 1932

    Omega Marine

    One of the very first watches designed for divers, with a sliding double case for better water resistance.

  • 1936

    The Italian commando watches

    Panerai equips the Italian navy’s combat swimmers with large, very luminous watches, legible in dark waters.

  • 1953

    Blancpain Fifty Fathoms

    Designed for the French Navy’s combat swimmers, at the request of officers Robert Maloubier and Claude Riffaud. It popularises the bezel that only turns one way. Some can be seen on the team’s wrists in The Silent World.

  • 1953

    Rolex Submariner

    Presented to the public in 1954, it sets the look of the dive watch for a long time: graduated bezel, large luminous indices, screw-down crown.

  • 1957

    Omega Seamaster 300

    Another pioneer of professional dive watches, valued by military and scientific divers.

  • 1965

    Seiko 62MAS

    The first Japanese dive watch, the start of a long line famous for its toughness and excellent value.

  • 1967

    Doxa SUB and its orange dial

    An orange dial, the color that stays most visible underwater, and a bezel engraved with a decompression scale. It is associated with the divers of the Cousteau team.

  • 1967

    Saturation dive watches

    With the professional divers of the French company COMEX came watches fitted with a helium valve, like the Rolex Sea-Dweller, then steel monsters like the Omega Ploprof in 1970.

  • 1969

    Certina DS and the Tektite aquanauts

    Certina relies on its “double security” (DS): a case and movement protected against water and shocks. Its DS-2 Super PH500M, then PH1000M, equip the aquanauts of Tektite, an underwater habitat programme supported notably by NASA, who live for weeks on the seabed of the Virgin Islands in 1969 and 1970.

  • 1975

    The “tuna can”

    Seiko wraps its professional watch in a protective shroud, earning it this affectionate nickname among divers.

  • 1985

    Citizen Aqualand, the Big Blue watch

    The world’s first watch with an electronic depth gauge: next to its hands, a display shows the depth reached. In 1988, a black version appears on the wrist of Jean Reno, as Enzo Molinari, in Luc Besson’s The Big Blue.

Since the 1980s, the ISO 6425 standard has set what a watch must prove to be called a “diver’s watch”: at least 100 metres of water resistance, a bezel or device to read dive time, legibility in the dark at 25 centimetres, and a series of resistance tests.

The unidirectional bezel

The diver’s basic tool, even before the computer.

When going under, the diver turns the bezel to place its triangle opposite the minute hand. At any moment, they can then read the elapsed time directly on the bezel.

It only turns one way, the one that reduces the remaining time: if knocked by mistake, it can only show a longer dive time than the real one, never a shorter one. An error on the safe side.

The MN90 tables

Breathing compressed air dissolves nitrogen in the body. If you come up too fast, this nitrogen forms bubbles: decompression sickness. To avoid it, divers follow tables showing, depending on depth and dive time, how fast to ascend and how long to stop at shallow depths.

In France, the reference tables are the MN90, drawn up by the French Navy and published in 1990. They rely on a mathematical model that imagines the body as twelve “compartments” loading and unloading nitrogen at different rates. They were adopted by the French underwater federation (FFESSM), which published an adapted version.

Depth and time
You enter the table with the maximum depth reached and the dive time, both rounded up.
The stops
The table shows the decompression stops to make, at 3, 6 or 9 metres and deeper, and their length.
Ascent speed
You ascend slowly, about 15 to 17 metres per minute to the first stop, then even more slowly between stops.
Repetitive dives
A letter, the repetitive dive group, shows the nitrogen left in the body after the dive and lets you plan a second dive the same day.

Dive computers

A table assumes a “square” dive: the whole time spent at maximum depth. In reality, divers keep going up and down along a reef wall. As early as 1959, an Italian analogue decompression meter tried to follow the actual dive, with mixed success.

In the early 1980s came the first reliable electronic dive computers. They measure depth and time continuously and recalculate the nitrogen in each compartment at every moment, using models such as that of Swiss physiologist Albert Bühlmann. They show the remaining no-stop time and the ascent speed, and warn the diver.

Today, they fit in a watch, handle oxygen-enriched mixes, can read cylinder pressure and connect to a phone. The mechanical dive watch doesn’t disappear for all that: it remains the backup instrument, with no battery, that works whatever happens.

In the fenua

French Polynesia is one of the most beautiful diving areas in the world: the passes of Rangiroa and Fakarava, with their walls of sharks and manta rays, draw divers from all over the planet.

But our islands also have a far older diving history. In the Tuamotu, pearl-shell divers went down holding their breath, without any equipment, to great depths to gather pearl oysters. The accidents that struck some after repeated dives even gave a word to diving medicine: taravana, “to fall crazily” in Paumotu.
