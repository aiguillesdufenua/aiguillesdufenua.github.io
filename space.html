Watches in space

In a vacuum, under extreme temperature swings, a watch can become a survival instrument. Here is the story of the watches that left Earth, and why good old hand-wound mechanics keep the trust of astronauts when they step out into space.

A space timeline

  1. 1961

    The first man in space

    Yuri Gagarin orbits the Earth aboard Vostok 1, wearing a watch from the First Moscow Watch Factory.

  2. 1962

    The first Americans in orbit

    John Glenn carries a stopwatch strapped to his suit; Scott Carpenter, a pilot’s chronograph with a 24-hour dial, to tell day from night in orbit. Wally Schirra wears his own chronograph, a Speedmaster bought in a shop.

  3. 1965

    The first walks in the void

    In March, NASA qualifies the Speedmaster for all its manned missions. That same month, Soviet cosmonaut Alexei Leonov makes the very first spacewalk, a Strela chronograph on his wrist. In June, Ed White makes the first American spacewalk, his Speedmaster strapped to his suit.

  4. 1969

    On the Moon

    On 21 July, Buzz Aldrin steps onto the Moon wearing his Speedmaster. Neil Armstrong had left his inside the lunar module as a backup, because an onboard clock had failed.

  5. 1970

    Apollo 13

    After an oxygen tank explodes, the crew must correct their course by hand. Jack Swigert times a 14-second engine burn to the second on his Speedmaster, bringing the spacecraft back on track.

  6. 1971

    David Scott’s Bulova

    The crystal of the Apollo 15 commander’s official watch having popped off, he wears a personal Bulova chronograph for his last moonwalk. The story is told below.

  7. 1973

    An automatic in orbit

    Aboard the Skylab station, William Pogue wears his own Seiko chronograph, without official approval. It is one of the very first automatic chronographs to go to space, and it works well on board, wound by the movements of his arm.

  8. 1981 – 2011

    The shuttle years

    Digital watches enter the spacecraft. Several Casio G-Shock models, including the famous DW-5600, are flight-qualified by NASA. Tough, light, with stopwatch and countdown timer, they become among the most popular with shuttle crews, then on the Space Station.

  9. 1998

    The quartz era

    The Speedmaster X-33, analogue and digital, is designed with astronauts for life aboard spacecraft and stations.

  10. Today

    The Space Station

    Aboard the ISS, astronauts wear all kinds of watches, from smartwatches to multifunction quartz. But for stepping out into the void, the hand-wound mechanical chronograph remains one of the safest choices.

NASA’s eleven trials

In 1964, NASA asked watchmakers for chronographs able to survive space. Three models from three houses went through eleven consecutive trials. Only one passed them all, on 1 March 1965. They also had to stay accurate to a few seconds a day, legible and anti-magnetic.

The Moonwatch

The chronograph NASA qualified in 1965 is a hand-wound model with a highly legible black dial, three sub-dials and a tachymeter bezel. The day it walked on the Moon, it earned the nickname it still carries: the Moonwatch.

Its crystal is hesalite, a plexiglass: on impact, it doesn’t shatter into shards that would float around the cabin in microgravity, a real danger to eyes and instruments. Its calibre has evolved over the decades, but it has stayed hand-wound, and the watch is still qualified for manned flight.

David Scott’s Bulova

July 1971: David Scott, commander of Apollo 15 and the seventh man to walk on the Moon, wears the NASA-issued Speedmaster like every astronaut. After his second moonwalk, he finds that his watch’s crystal has popped off, probably because of the pressure difference between the inside of the watch and the vacuum.

On 2 August, for his third and final moonwalk, he straps on his backup chronograph, a Bulova prototype entrusted to him before the mission. NASA, which had not authorised it, later called it an “unauthorized timepiece” and kept its brand quiet for years.

Remaining the astronaut’s property, whereas NASA watches belong to the government, it is the only Moon-worn watch in private hands. It sold at auction in October 2015 for 1.625 million dollars, with its Velcro strap still marked by lunar dust. Bulova has since reissued it as the Lunar Pilot.

The X-33: quartz aboard spacecraft

In the late 1990s, the Speedmaster X-33 was developed with astronauts and pilots. It combines hands with two digital displays and packs in functions useful on board: mission elapsed time (MET), several time zones, very loud alarms, chronographs and countdowns, backlighting. A version developed in 2014 with the European Space Agency adds even more.

It wasn’t alone: well before it, several Casio G-Shocks had been flight-qualified by NASA, and the National Air and Space Museum in Washington counts them among the most popular watches of the shuttle era. Their stopwatch and countdown timer helped crews follow a schedule timed to the minute.

The X-33 has been worn on the Mir station and aboard the ISS. Inside a spacecraft, at a stable temperature, these digital tools are invaluable for following a schedule timed to the minute. But as soon as it comes to stepping out into the void, the arguments change.

Why hand-wound mechanics still reign on EVA

During a spacewalk, the watch is strapped over the suit with a long Velcro strap. It serves as a backup timer, independent of every electronic system. Here is why a hand-wound mechanical chronograph suits that role so well.

  • A rotor without gravity

    An automatic watch’s rotor turns mainly thanks to gravity. In microgravity, it only benefits from the arm’s accelerations: on board, William Pogue’s Seiko worked well that way, but in a stiff suit, with slow and constrained gestures, winding becomes uncertain. A hand-wound watch is wound with a few turns before going out, with complete certainty.

  • Extreme temperatures

    In sunlight, with no atmosphere, a surface exceeds 100 °C; in shade, it plunges below −100 °C. A well-designed mechanical movement copes, while a battery loses power in the cold and a liquid crystal display can freeze or go black.

  • No battery, no electronics

    In space, cosmic radiation can disrupt electronic circuits. A mechanical watch has nothing to upset on that side, and no battery to replace: as long as it is wound, it runs.

  • Usable with gloves

    Large hands and a high-contrast dial can be read at a glance through a visor; big pushers can be operated with pressurised gloves, which is much harder with tiny buttons and digital menus.

  • A crystal that doesn’t shatter

    The Moonwatch’s hesalite crystal can scratch, but it doesn’t break into splinters: in microgravity, shards of glass floating in a cabin would be dangerous.

  • Proven trust

    Sixty years of missions, passed qualification trials, and stories like Apollo 13: for a backup instrument a life may depend on, that demonstrated reliability counts for a great deal.

This doesn’t mean quartz is absent from space: on board, digital and smart watches are everywhere, and the suit has its own instruments. But when it comes to having a simple, autonomous, proven backup timer on the wrist, hand-wound mechanics keep their place.

In the fenua

On a clear night, look up: the International Space Station regularly passes over Tahiti. It appears as a very bright dot crossing the sky in a few minutes, without blinking. And perhaps, on board, a hand-wound watch is waiting for the next spacewalk.
